# The State of the Law

<Disclaimer />

## The doctrinal shift: *Chatrie v. United States*

On June 29, 2026, the Supreme Court decided
[*Chatrie v. United States*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-112_0am4.pdf),
609 U.S. \_\_\_ (No. 25-112) — a 6–3 decision written by Justice Kagan.

The Court held that when police obtain a person's cell-phone location
history from Google via a geofence warrant, that is a **Fourth Amendment
search**, even though the data is held by a third party (Google). The
government had argued that under the traditional "third-party doctrine,"
people give up their expectation of privacy in data they voluntarily hand
to a company. The Court rejected that argument for location history,
reasoning that people don't meaningfully choose to expose a detailed record
of their movements just by carrying a phone.

Having decided that acquiring the data was a search, the Court did **not**
go on to decide whether the geofence warrant in this case actually satisfied
the Fourth Amendment's particularity and probable-cause requirements. It
vacated the Fourth Circuit's judgment and remanded the case for that
question to be resolved on remand.

**What *Chatrie* did not do:** it is not a ruling about automated license
plate readers or about Flock Safety specifically. No ALPR case has reached
the Supreme Court.

## Why it matters for the ALPR fight anyway

*Chatrie* matters here because it is the clearest sign yet that the Supreme
Court is willing to extend Fourth Amendment protection to location data held
by third parties, rejecting a narrow reading of the third-party doctrine.
ALPR networks — especially cross-jurisdictional systems that let one agency
query another agency's camera data, or nationwide lookups spanning tens of
thousands of cameras — raise a similar question: does a driver have a
reasonable expectation of privacy in the aggregate record of everywhere
their car has been photographed, even though each individual photograph was
taken in public?

The ALPR cases moving through lower courts right now (see the
[legal tracker](/legal/)) are, in large part, arguments that the reasoning
in *Chatrie* — and in the location-data cases before it — should extend to
license plate data. None of them has been decided by the Supreme Court, and
*Chatrie* itself did not rule on that question. Treat it as the doctrinal
foundation those arguments are being built on, not as a ruling against Flock
Safety or any ALPR vendor.

## Where things stand

- **Geofence/location data (cell phones):** *Chatrie* establishes that
  acquiring this data is a search. Warrant particularity/probable-cause
  standards for geofence warrants are still being worked out on remand.
- **ALPR data:** unsettled. Multiple suits are testing whether warrantless
  ALPR queries, especially cross-agency or out-of-state sharing, violate the
  Fourth Amendment or state law. See the [legal tracker](/legal/) for the
  current status of each.
